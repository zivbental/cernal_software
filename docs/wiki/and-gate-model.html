The two-input AND gate: what 301,440 designs say

CERNAL model page · Results and Discussion · characterisation run axes_c8

A one-input toehold switch reports one gene. A transcriptomic signature is a combination of genes, so the circuit needs logic. This section reports what we measured when we enumerated and folded the two-input AND gate across its whole design grid, and what those measurements say about whether the architecture works the way it was designed to.

Results

R0 · The quantities this section uses, and where they come from

Four tubes are folded for every design, named by which triggers are present with trigger A as the first digit: 00 the switch alone, 01 switch + trigger B, 10 switch + trigger A, 11 switch + both. 11 is the ON state and the other three are OFF states.

dG_open_11 — the ON state's cost. Around the start codon there is a 31-nt window, offsets −17 to +13 counting the A of AUG as +1, which is the footprint a ribosome has to find unpaired in order to initiate. dG_open_11 is the free energy, in kcal/mol, of forcing that whole window open in the ON tube: how much the structure has to be paid to let the ribosome in once both triggers are bound. Lower is better.

Why that window, and where the threshold came from. The window is the span Green et al. (2014) report their switch performance over, which is what makes the number comparable to measured constructs rather than internal to our model. The threshold was taken from their data directly: 7.2 kcal/mol is the highest ON cost among the 77 of their switches that achieve more than 20-fold ON/OFF, against a median of 3.64 across all 168. A design whose ON state costs more than that to open is one no measured working switch resembles.

"Worst OFF" — and why a contrast is taken against it. A gate is only as good as its leakiest OFF state, so every contrast compares the ON state against whichever of 00, 01 and 10 is worst for the gate — not an average of them. Worst means most open: for an opening cost it is the OFF state with the lowest cost, because that is the one that opens most easily; for an accessibility it is the highest value; for a defect, the least paired. Which of the three actually turns out to be the worst is itself a result, and §R2 reports it.

What filters state 10: nothing, in this run. The ON-state ceiling (§R1) is the only filter applied here, and it looks exclusively at the ON state. The state-10 leak is measured on every design and never filtered on. This matters for reading §R3: the ceiling is not independent of the leak it does not look at, so a population shaped by the ceiling is already shaped, indirectly, by the leak. The later selection stage does have state-referenced floors — lock and lock01 on how free the nucleation site is, hairpin_worst, engaged_arm_11, aug_11 — but none of them ran here, which is deliberate: a characterisation run that filtered on its own outcome could not be used to study that outcome.

R1 · The dataset, and the questions it can and cannot answer

The run enumerated 301,440 designs over 210 trigger pairs drawn from three PFAS-responsive E. coli gene pairs (ggt–fadB, ggt–astC, ggt–aldA), in two switch geometries — 420 cells, where a cell is one trigger pair in one geometry. 219,808 designs (72.9%) were measured in all four tube states; the remaining 27.1% were stopped by the ON-state ceiling and carry their rejection reason rather than being dropped.

Every comparison below is paired inside a cell. A cell fixes both trigger windows and the geometry, so within one cell the only thing that differs is the design decision. This matters quantitatively, not just in principle: between-pair variation is far larger than any lever's effect, and pooling across cells measures the trigger pairs instead of the design.

The design grid is 16 start-codon closures × 3 upper-stem settings × 2 lower-stem settings = 96 points per cell. 276 of 420 cells (65.7%) hold the complete grid; 4,736 of 40,320 points (11.7%) are absent, and the absence is not spread evenly — 82.4% of it is one upper-stem level, WWW_AUA, which generate_designs declines for 122 of 420 cells (≈29% of trigger pairs).

The reason is specific and worth stating, because "a level is missing" otherwise looks like unfinished work. The upper-stem lever writes its reverse complement into main_z — the three nucleotides of spacer immediately 5′ of the start codon. Assembly has already run a repair over that spacer to remove any out-of-frame AUG from the 5′ UTR, because a ribosome that initiates there reads the payload in the wrong frame. Overwriting those three bases can reintroduce one. The repair then runs again, and it may only substitute bases that still pair with their partner in k1* (wobbles allowed, so the stem stays shut); when no such substitution removes the AUG, the design is dropped with the reason aug_utr_unrepairable rather than emitted in the wrong frame. Whether a repair exists depends on k1*, which is trigger-derived — hence the pair-to-pair variation. WWW_AUA writes UAU into the spacer and WWW_UAU writes AUA; only the first completes an out-of-frame AUG against the fixed bases before it, which is why one level loses 3,904 points and the other loses none. The base-level confirmation is recorded as still open in the findings register. Closure and lower-stem levels are exactly balanced (18,840 and 150,720 rows per level), so no closure or lower-stem comparison is affected. The least-covered cells hold 32 of 96 points, i.e. a single upper-stem level.

Provenance is uniform: all 420 cells were built under one setting (stem_order=front_tops, 6 stems per pair, overlap 4–18 nt, pair_order=spread), so a within-cell comparison is sound. The ON ceiling actually applied was open_11 ≤ 8.0 kcal/mol — the main stem's 15-nt arms, which is the span the engine version that built this table used, not the 31-nt ribosome window of §R0. The 15 nt sit inside the 31, so the narrower test is the looser one. §R8 establishes which column the ceiling was read against from the data rather than assuming it, and re-applies Green's own dG_open_11 ≤ 7.2 line to this population as a counterfactual.

What the filter boundary allows. dG_open_11 and the two state-10 leak terms are measured on every design, before the ceiling decides, so results about them hold over all 301,440 designs with no survivorship conditioning. The three OFF tubes are only folded once a design passes the ceiling, so every contrast — and every worst-OFF term — exists only on the 219,808 survivors. Those results are conditional on survival, and since survival differs by a factor of two between closure levels, that conditioning is not a technicality. The two kinds of claim are kept apart throughout.

R2 · State 10 is the state the gate is judged on

The architecture's premise is that trigger A alone must not open the gate: state 10 is what separates a two-input AND from a one-input switch. We can now say how often that premise is the binding constraint rather than an assumption. For each design we recorded which of the three OFF states the ON/OFF contrast was actually taken against.

So the quality of a two-input gate is, to a very good approximation, a question about one tube. This is also what makes the state-10 leak worth measuring directly, which we do on every design: xstar_engA_10 is the probability that trigger A, on its own, occupies the nucleation site it should not be able to reach until trigger B has released it.

Over all 301,440 designs its median is 0.9988 and only 23.4% of designs keep it below 0.5. The companion term mainpre_engA_10 — trigger A alone reaching the main stem — has median 0.9761, with 28.8% below 0.5. The leak is the normal case, not the exception. Consistently, the median hairpin_worst_10 across measured designs is 1.8 × 10⁻⁶: in half of them the hairpins essentially do not hold once trigger A is present.

R3 · The design levers act on the leak, and not on the ON state

Each ON/OFF contrast is built from two measured terms — an ON-state value and the worst of the three OFF states. Reporting only the contrast cannot distinguish a gate that turns on better from a gate that leaks less, and for a ratio it cannot distinguish either from a collapsing denominator. Reported separately, the two sides behave completely differently.

The reading is unambiguous. The secondary stem's energies move the leak, by a large margin; they move the worst OFF state moderately; and they do not move the ON state at all — every ON-state term sits at |ρ| ≤ 0.05 while the leak reaches 0.70.

So what earlier single-number reports read as "this design decision gates better" is, when decomposed, "this design decision leaks less". The ON state never improved.

The last column is the engine's own ied_gain, which references state 00 alone — D3 shows that is the one definition of "the OFF state" that does not work for this architecture, so read that column as a property of the definition and not of the ensemble defect. Referenced to the worst OFF state instead, the defect behaves like the other contrasts (§R5).

Every lever reduces the state-10 leak almost universally — wobble_GU wins 171 cells and loses none — and improves separation. Every lever also costs ON-state openness in roughly two-thirds of cells. That trade is the same one R3 describes, seen from the lever side rather than the energy side.

Two further features matter for how these numbers should be read. First, the median paired difference is +0.0000 for almost every lever and outcome while the mean is clearly positive: the levers do nothing to most designs and a great deal to a minority. Second, the effects are threshold-shaped, not gradients, which a correlation coefficient cannot show.

The method, in plain terms. Think of each cell as a school class: all the designs in it are built on the same two trigger sequences, so they sat the same exam. Classes differ enormously in difficulty — in some trigger pairs nothing works at all, in others almost everything does (§R9) — so a raw score from one class cannot be compared with a raw score from another.

  1. Sort within the class. Inside each cell, order the designs by how tightly trigger A grips the secondary arm, and split them into five equal groups. Q1 is the fifth with the tightest grip, Q5 the fifth with the weakest. Each class contributes a fifth of its designs to each group.
  2. Grade on the class curve. Replace each design's raw result with "how far from its own class average, in units of that class's own spread". A value of −1 means one spread better than that class's typical design; 0 means exactly typical. This is what makes a result from an easy class comparable with one from a hard class, and it is all a z-score is.
  3. Average each group across all classes. That gives the five numbers in the table.

Splitting inside each class is the part that matters. If instead we cut on the raw energy across everything — say "everything below −30 kcal/mol goes in group 1" — then a trigger pair whose designs all happen to sit below −30 would land in group 1 entirely, and an easy pair might land entirely in group 5. The groups would then differ by which pairs are in them, and we would be measuring the pairs rather than the grip. Cutting inside the class forces every group to contain a fair mix of easy and hard pairs, so the only thing that separates the groups is the axis being studied.

So the design rule has a number attached to it. Trigger A's grip on the secondary arm has to be weaker than about −15 to −17 kcal/mol. Stronger than that, the leak is saturated and no amount of further tightening or loosening inside that range changes anything — which is why a correlation coefficient, which assumes a trend, reported a moderate number for what is really a cliff with a flat approach.

The other two contrasts are plotted on the same axis, because a shape seen on one contrast and not the others would be a property of that contrast rather than of the design.

R6 · Closure: survival and gating run in opposite directions

All sixteen start-codon closures were swept, including the ten systematic (depth × G+C) levels that had never reached a scored population before. Survival through the ON ceiling and the rate at which survivors actually gate are anti-correlated.

closed_CAU survives at 40.7% and gates 26.9% of its survivors; open_3x3 survives at 82.9% and gates 9.5%. A closure that keeps more designs alive is simply one whose ON state opens more easily — which is not the same thing as a working gate, and on this axis is close to its opposite.

R7 · Does one lever's effect depend on another's setting?

An interaction means the answer to "is this lever good?" changes depending on how a different lever is set. That is the question behind "if the secondary hairpin is locked hard, should the main one be closed hard too?", and a single-axis table cannot answer it: it averages over the other lever and reports one number.

The largest apparent interaction here is start-codon closure × secondary-stem scheme. Among B-anchored designs, closing the start codon helps about four times as much as it does among mixed designs: with closed_CAU the mean within-cell z is −1.37 for B-anchored against −0.32 for mixed. With the open closure the two schemes are nearly identical (+0.30 against +0.39). Read naively, that says the closure lever and the scheme lever reinforce each other.

But the two schemes are not otherwise alike. They build the secondary arm differently, so they have systematically different a_site_energy — and §R4 showed a_site_energy is the strongest axis in the study. The apparent interaction could therefore be that axis wearing a label: B-anchored designs may simply be the strong-grip designs, and what looks like "closure works better with B-anchored" may be "closure works better when the grip is strong".

The test is to hold the energy fixed. Designs are split into four groups by a_site_energy, and the same closure-by-scheme comparison is recomputed inside each group, where the two schemes now have nearly the same grip:

The gap collapses — 0.71, 0.48, 0.07, −0.02. Once grip strength is held fixed, the two schemes behave almost identically, and in the weak-grip group they are indistinguishable. So most of what looked like a closure × scheme interaction was a_site_energy under another name. Something survives at the tightest grip, but on a few hundred designs against several thousand, so it is a lead rather than a finding. This also explains why scheme passes every sensitivity test in §R8: it is standing in for the study's strongest axis.

One real interaction does survive, and it is a disagreement between objectives. With a strongly closed start codon, the two contrasts order the upper-stem lever oppositely: for sep_a_m the best combination is closed_UAU with the natural upper stem (−1.13 against −0.55 for the WWW_AUA override), while for sep_open the same closure prefers the override (−0.56 against −0.28). There is therefore no upper-stem setting that is best irrespective of which objective is being optimised — the choice has to follow from deciding what the gate is for.

Does it hold at Green's actual threshold?

The criterion is Green's, but this table applied it over the narrower 15-nt span (§R1). Because dG_open_11 is recorded on every design, the question can simply be asked: re-filter this population at Green's own line and re-derive the rankings. At dG_open_11 ≤ 7.06 — essentially his 7.2 — 159,083 designs survive across 333 cells, and:

  • scheme = B-anchored is still best for all four contrasts across the whole swept range, ordering intact;
  • lower3 and upper3 keep the same winners as above, also with their orderings intact;
  • closure flips more readily between the strongly-closed levels, as it does under the applied ceiling.

So the conclusions do not depend on which span the ceiling was read over. The selection can be described as calibrated against Green's measured switches without qualification: the claim has been tested on this population rather than inherited from the metric's provenance. What the narrower span changed is which designs were measured, not which design decisions win.

Applying the selection-stage floors instead changes between three and five of the twenty conclusions each, concentrated in sep_ied and in geom. Under the two leak floors sep_ied reverses its upper- and lower-stem winners, which is consistent with §R3: conditioning on low leak removes exactly the variation those levers act on.

R9 · The pair decides whether the gate can open; the design decides whether it leaks

Of 210 trigger pairs, 15 (7.1%) have no design that opens at all and 75 (35.7%) have more than 95% of their designs open; median per-pair survival is 0.880. The distribution is strongly U-shaped — 22 pairs in the 0–10% band, 98 in the 90–100% band, few in between. A smooth spread would mean every pair is partially workable and the levers decide how far you get. A U means a pair is mostly either usable or dead, and which one it is was settled before any design decision was made. Pair screening is a pass/fail gate, not a ranking.

But "the pair dominates" turns out to be true of one thing and false of another, and the distinction is the most useful result in this section. Every other statistic on this page is a within-cell z-score, which subtracts the pair on purpose and therefore cannot measure it. Splitting the raw variance instead — how much lies between trigger pairs against how much lies within one pair, across its designs — gives this:

The split is clean and it runs along the same seam as §R3. Two thirds of the variance in the ON-state opening cost is between trigger pairs — whether a gate built on this pair can turn on is mostly decided by the pair, and the designer has roughly a third of it to play with. For the leak and every contrast, 86% to 98% of the variance is within a pair — the pair barely constrains how well the gate discriminates, and that is almost entirely the designer's to win or lose.

This also corrects the natural reading of the U-shaped survival curve. The pair decides whether there is anything to work with; it does not decide how good the result will be. The two questions need separate screens, and the second one is where the design levers earn their keep.

Consistently, the pair's own local accessibility l_local_min correlates with the cell's best achievable ON-state openness (ρ −0.191 on A_M_11, p = 1.7 × 10⁻⁴; −0.188 on dG_open_11) and with no contrast at all (|ρ| ≤ 0.14). Accessibility predicts whether a gate can turn on and says nothing about whether it will discriminate — the same division, reached by a different route.

Why accessibility should touch the ON state at all is worth stating as a hypothesis, because it is not obvious and we have not tested it. The switch is not an independent molecule: its stems are built as reverse complements of the trigger segments. A trigger window that is strongly self-paired inside its own transcript is one whose sequence has a high intrinsic tendency to pair — typically GC-rich, or carrying self-complementary runs. Copy that sequence into the switch and it makes a more stable hairpin, which is harder to open in the ON state. On that account the correlation is sequence composition propagating from the trigger into the switch, and GC content is the obvious mediator.

The test has to be per trigger, not on the pair's minimum. Using l_local_min throws away which of the two triggers it came from, and the two can differ sharply in composition. Matching each trigger to "its" hairpin does not work either, because the secondary hairpin is not one trigger's: its arm carries x*, which is simultaneously the site trigger A must nucleate on and the segment trigger B invades, so both triggers' sequences are present in that one structure — which is exactly why the architecture needs two separate energies to describe it. The right unit is the measured term, since each already names whose binding it describes: a_site_energy against l_local_a, b_site_energy against l_local_b, and lock_energy against both, where the expectation is that neither alone accounts for it. Then ask whether each link survives holding that trigger's own GC content fixed.

The test, run. It needed no re-folding — both accessibilities and all three stem energies are already on every row, and GC comes from re-slicing the two footprints out of the reference. The unit is one trigger pair in one geometry, with the stem energies taken as the median over that cell's stems; per-stem rows would count a pair once per usable stem, and how many stems a pair yields is a property of the pair, so the significance would have been inflated by exactly the thing under test.

A positive ρ is the direction the hypothesis predicts: openness is 0–1 with higher meaning more accessible, the energies are kcal/mol with lower meaning tighter, so "a less accessible window binds more tightly" reads as positive. Both accessibilities and all three energies are pre-filter columns, so nothing here is conditioned on survival. One caveat on the p values: each trigger pair contributes two cells, one per geometry, with near-identical windows, so the effective sample is nearer 210 than 420 and every p above is roughly twice as optimistic as it looks. Every result survives that correction except l_local_b → a_site_energy, which becomes borderline (p ≈ 0.05).

The hypothesis holds, and it holds per trigger. Each stem energy is predicted by its own trigger's accessibility, and in both cases the own-trigger link is the stronger of the two. For trigger B it is the only one: b_site_energy is flat against l_local_a (ρ −0.020, p 0.69). So l_local_min really was throwing information away, and the per-trigger form was the right test.

The asymmetry is the architecture, not noise. a_site_energy carries some of trigger B's accessibility (+0.135) while b_site_energy carries none of A's. That is what the shared x* predicts: a_site_energy describes trigger A binding the secondary arm, and that arm is built from x*, which is read out of gene B. A term for A's binding inherits B's composition, because the thing A binds is B's sequence.

And the expectation about lock_energy was wrong, informatively. We expected neither accessibility alone to account for the secondary stem's own stability, since both triggers' sequences are present in that structure. Measured, it is trigger B's alone — ρ +0.320 against l_local_b, +0.036 and not significant against l_local_a. Same reason: the stem is the x* side, so its composition comes from gene B. The secondary hairpin is mixed in function and single-sided in sequence, and only the second of those shows up in an energy.

Is GC the mediator? Almost entirely for A, mostly for B. GC tracks both halves strongly and in the predicted directions — gc_a → l_local_a ρ −0.549 (GC-rich is less accessible) and gc_a → a_site_energy ρ −0.571 (GC-rich binds tighter); for B, −0.465 and −0.355. For trigger A the link collapses: six-sevenths of it is GC propagating from the transcript into the switch, and what is left changes sign between strata, which is what no effect looks like. The precise statement matters here. Accessibility and GC are not interchangeable in general — they share under a third of their rank variance — but the part of accessibility that predicts a stem energy is almost all its GC part. The §R9 hypothesis is confirmed and then largely explained away: the mechanism is sequence composition propagating from the transcript into the switch, not accessibility as such. Which also makes the pair-level link this section opened with — l_local_min against the cell's best A_M_11, ρ −0.191 — likely a composition effect at one remove rather than an accessibility effect. That is the same test on a different outcome and we have not run it.

R10 · Loose ends: one naming defect, and one question now closed

One quantity is measured under two names, and the second name is empty. Trigger accessibility — how exposed a trigger's window is inside its own transcript — appears in this run twice over:

  • l_local_a, l_local_b and their minimum l_local_min, from RNAplfold over a sliding window. Filled on all 301,440 rows, per trigger rather than per pair, and §R9 is built on them.
  • access_a and access_b, the same property priced as an energy — the engine's own documentation calls it "p_open expressed as an energy rather than a probability", chosen because the probability underflows in the OFF states. Empty on all 301,440 rows: measure_design computes them only when handed the source transcript and both trigger sites, and the runner does not pass them. It returns None, correctly, rather than a zero that would have read as a perfect score.

So nothing is missing from this study — the property it would have measured is measured, and the analysis that needs it has it. The gap is a naming one, and this project treats that as a defect in its own right: two implementations of one measurement produce two numbers and only one reaches the report. The decision to make is which of the two is canonical, and then to retire the other. Computing both and carrying both is the one option that should be ruled out.

The Kim-versus-naive geometry comparison is now made, and the answer is that it makes no difference. geom is part of the cell key, so a within-cell z-score can never compare the two geometries — every × geom table in this analysis, and every geometry flip under the sensitivity sweeps, is an artefact of that. But the comparison was always available one level up: each trigger pair contributes two cells, one per geometry, built on the same two windows, and every design in one has a counterpart in the other sharing its whole axis tuple. Matching those gives 140,256 matched design pairs differing in geometry alone, across 195 trigger pairs.

Nothing reaches significance, the median paired difference is exactly 0.000 on all four, and the four disagree with each other — the two opening contrasts lean to the naive arm, the other two to Kim's. That pattern is what noise looks like. On the opening contrasts, 72 of 195 pairs are exactly tied: for a third of trigger pairs the geometry changes the result not at all. Survival is the same story — of 140,256 matched pairs, 997 open only under Kim's geometry and 629 only under the naive one, against 101,595 that open under both.

So Kim's extended secondary arm — 20 nt against 18, invasion 17 against 18, an AUA cap — buys nothing measurable on this architecture. That is a useful negative result rather than an absence of one: it says the extra geometry is not where the gate's problem lies, and the design effort belongs on the levers §R8 shows to be robust.

Discussion and Conclusions

D1 · What the architecture is actually doing

The design intent was a gate held shut by two independent locks: a main hairpin sequestering the RBS and start codon, and a secondary hairpin holding the domain trigger A needs, so that neither input alone produces output. The measurements support the premise and complicate the mechanism.

The premise holds: state 10 is the binding constraint in 80–99% of designs, so the gate really is judged on whether trigger A alone can open it. But at the population level trigger A alone can — the median probability that it occupies the nucleation site unaided is 0.9988, and only 23.4% of designs hold it below one half. The secondary hairpin, as currently designed, is not a lock that trigger A cannot pick; it is a lock trigger A usually picks, and the design problem is finding the minority of configurations where it does not.

That reframing is the most useful thing in this run. It converts the question from "how do we improve the gate" into "which configurations are in the 23%", which is a different and more tractable search.

D2 · The design rule, and the premise it undermines

The rule the data supports is clear and slightly uncomfortable: make the secondary stem as stable as possible against itself, and let trigger A grip it as weakly as possible. lock_minus_a_site captures both and is the strongest predictor in the study.

The discomfort is this. The architecture's rationale for trigger A binding the secondary arm is that A needs the help: the secondary hairpin holds the domain A nucleates on, so A's affinity for that arm is part of how the gate turns on in state 11. The decomposition says that affinity buys no measurable ON-state opening — every ON-state term is flat against it, |ρ| ≤ 0.05 — while it is the single largest driver of the state-10 leak, at ρ 0.70. On the evidence available, trigger A's grip on the secondary arm is a cost with no detectable benefit.

We state that carefully, because it is a negative result about a measurement rather than about the chemistry. The ON state may well depend on A's grip in ways our ON-state metrics do not capture — all four of them are equilibrium ensemble properties of the fully-assembled three-strand tube. A term that controls the rate of opening in state 11 would not necessarily show up in any of them.

There is one kinetic observable on every measured row, and it does not rescue the grip. barrier is a findpath activation barrier in kcal/mol above the starting structure — the highest energy on a path between two structures, which is a different quantity from the difference between their energies and the only thing in this engine that is a barrier at all. On one real 165-nt switch the state difference is +8.59 kcal/mol while the barrier is +9.60, so it is not a rescaled difference. Against the stem energies it is flat in the same way every equilibrium ON-state term is: median within-cell ρ −0.021 against a_site_energy and +0.024 against lock_energy over 388 cells. The sign is consistent (291 of 388 cells negative), so the effect is real and reliably detectable; its size is negligible. That strengthens D2 rather than weakening it — the one non-equilibrium quantity we have agrees with the four equilibrium ones.

It is not, however, the measurement that would settle the question, and the reason is worth stating because it is the next thing to build. barrier is computed on the switch strand alone, from its own MFE structure to the same structure with the main stem's arms released: the wall the molecule clears once a trigger has already committed. No trigger is in the path, because findpath takes one strand and does not accept the & that joins two molecules — so the quantity that would actually test whether A's grip buys ON-state performance, the barrier along the invasion path in state 11, is not what this column holds. It is also an upper bound rather than the true saddle, since findpath walks a heuristic direct path, and it is measured only on designs that passed the ON ceiling.

D3 · A metric's reference state is a design decision, not a detail

The IED gain illustrates a general hazard, and the point is about the reference state and not about the ensemble defect. The defect itself is one of the three contrasts this study is built on and it is informative: sep_ied tracks a_site_energy at ρ +0.620, ranks the levers the same way the opening contrasts do (§R5), and survives the threshold sweeps (§R8). What fails is one particular choice of what to compare the ON state against. Three defensible definitions of "the OFF state" give three different answers from the same folded data:

Signs as measured on the raw columns. The three differ only in which states count as OFF.

So the measurement is fine and one of its three framings is not. The 00-referenced version is nearly uninformative and points the other way; the worst-OFF versions are strong and agree with each other. The reason is R2: state 10 binds the contrast, so a metric that references state 00 is measuring the resting structure and says almost nothing about AND behaviour. Including state 00 in the worst-of makes little difference precisely because it so rarely binds.

The practical consequence is that ied_gain should not be used as a selection metric for this architecture, and the engine's asymmetry — ied_off_worst excludes state 00 while the A_M, aug and meanW worst-OFF terms include it — should be resolved deliberately rather than left as an undocumented difference. On this evidence the worst-of-all-three convention is the right one for every family.

D4 · What this study cannot validate

No measured two-input performance exists for any construct we can score, and this needs saying plainly rather than in a footnote.

Green et al. (2014) and the VISTA library are single-input toehold switches — 370 constructs with measured ON/OFF between them. There is no second trigger, so AND-ness does not exist there and our contrasts can only be evaluated as their two-state analogues. A fair analogue is not the same function. Kim et al. (2019) is two-input and is the closest published relative of this architecture, but reports qualitative behaviour rather than ON/OFF ratios, so it supports a discrimination test and not a correlation.

Everything in R3–R9 is therefore internal consistency: relationships among our own computed quantities on our own designs. That is a legitimate and necessary thing to establish — a model whose metrics contradict each other cannot be right — but it is not evidence that the metrics predict function. The study's strongest claims are the ones that do not need calibration: that state 10 binds the contrast, that the leak is widespread, that the ON state does not respond to the stem energies. These are statements about what the folding model says, and they would have to be wrong in the model before they could be wrong in the cell.

D5 · Assumptions, simplifications and limits

Everything above rests on a model of the molecules, not on the molecules. These are the places where the model is knowingly simpler than the cell, listed so that a reader can tell which conclusions would survive a better model and which are artefacts of this one.

  • Equilibrium thermodynamics at 37 °C, ViennaRNA defaults. Every number on this page is an ensemble property of the nearest-neighbour energy model. One FoldEngine at one temperature produced all of them, carried on an explicit model object rather than a process-global setting, so no two designs were folded under different parameters — but a nearest-neighbour model at a single temperature is still the assumption underneath all 301,440 rows.
  • No concentrations, and therefore no expression levels. Each of the four tubes is the ensemble of one fixed complex: switch alone, switch&B, switch&A, switch&A&B. Nothing in it depends on how much trigger is present, so a trigger at one copy per cell and at a thousand give the same number. Measured ON/OFF depends heavily on that, and the model is silent on it. The same simplification makes state 10 "A present, B absent" rather than "A winning a competition against B".
  • No ribosome. The start-codon region's availability stands in for initiation. The 31-nt window is chosen for the 30S subunit's footprint, but no initiating ribosome, no standby binding and no competition between the ribosome and the structure is simulated. A gate that is thermodynamically open but kinetically inaccessible to 30S would score well here.
  • Almost no kinetics. One observable (barrier, D2) is a rate proxy, it is an upper bound on a heuristic path, and it is computed on the switch alone. The architecture is a branch-migration mechanism with an intended order of events, and nothing here measures how fast any step is.
  • The trigger is its window, folded free. Triggers enter the tubes as the footprints the pair record defines — 35–36 nt for A and 49–50 nt for B, depending on the geometry — as free strands. In the cell they are embedded in full transcripts; that is what l_local_a/l_local_b are for, and they enter the selection of pairs, not the folding of the tubes.
  • Trigger pairs are exact reverse-complementary runs. The search extends a perfect complement as far as it goes and stops; mismatched, bulged or wobble-tolerant pairings are outside the candidate space by construction, which almost certainly makes the pair pool smaller than the biology allows.
  • "Worst OFF" is a worst case, not an expectation. Every contrast judges a gate by its leakiest OFF state. A cell visits the three OFF states with different frequencies, and an expression-weighted criterion would rank designs differently. The choice is defensible for a logic gate and it is still a choice (D3).
  • Three gene pairs that are not independent. ggt is one half of all three (ggt/fadB, ggt/astC, ggt/aldA), and the role swap means it supplies trigger A in some pairs and trigger B in others. So the "three pairs" share one transcript, and the 210 trigger pairs drawn from them share far more sequence than three independent gene pairs would.
  • The within-cell z-score removes the trigger pair on purpose. Every lever number on this page is "relative to the other designs on the same pair". That is what makes the design effects visible and it is also what makes those numbers structurally unable to say how much the pair decides — §R9 is the only place that question is asked, and it needed a different statistic.
  • No off-target or host context. The run scores one switch against its own two triggers. Cross-talk with the rest of the transcriptome, RNase turnover, growth-rate effects and plasmid burden are all outside it.

And these are limits of the study rather than of the model:

  • The OFF side is conditional on survival. Every contrast exists only for designs that passed the ON ceiling, and survival varies twofold across closure levels. A lever that looks good on a contrast may be one whose bad designs were removed before the OFF states were folded. Only the ON-state and leak terms escape this.
  • One third of cells hold an incomplete grid, almost entirely because one upper-stem level is unbuildable for some trigger windows. Closure and lower-stem conclusions are unaffected; upper-stem conclusions rest on fewer matched pairs than the totals suggest.
  • The four contrasts are not independent. Within cells, sep_open and sep_dg_open agree at ρ 0.915 and sep_a_m and sep_ied at ρ 0.899. They should be read as two views, not four.
  • Three gene pairs, one organism, one stressor. 210 trigger pairs from three PFAS-responsive pairs in E. coli. R9 shows the pair matters more than the design, which makes the narrowness of the pair sample the single largest threat to generality.
  • Equilibrium only. Every observable is an ensemble property. The architecture is a branch-migration mechanism with an intended order of events (00 → 01 → 11), and nothing here measures a rate.
  • The population was shaped by a ceiling we would no longer apply. This table filtered on open_11 ≤ 8.0, the main stem's 15-nt arms; Green's criterion as the engine now evaluates it is dG_open_11 ≤ 7.2 over the 31-nt window. §R8 re-applies the latter as a counterfactual and the lever conclusions hold, but a design the older ceiling rejected was never folded in its OFF states, so no filter sweep can recover it.

D6 · Conclusions

  1. State 10 is the gate. It binds the ON/OFF contrast in 80–99% of designs. Any metric, filter or report for this architecture that does not reference state 10 is measuring something else.
  2. The current design leaks by default. Median unaided occupancy of the nucleation site by trigger A is 0.9988; 23.4% of designs hold it below one half. Design selection is a search for that minority.
  3. Lock hard, grip weakly. lock_minus_a_site is the strongest predictor available (ρ up to 0.785, 384/388 cells). A strongly closed start codon plus the wobble_GU lower stem plus a B-anchored secondary stem is the configuration that survives every threshold we can vary.
  4. The levers buy leak reduction and cost ON-state openness. They are not free, and they act on a minority of designs in a threshold-like way, so a design pool should be enumerated rather than nudged.
  5. Report closure on gating, never on survival. The two run opposite on this axis (40.7%/26.9% against 82.9%/9.5%).
  6. Screen the pair for whether it can open; design for whether it leaks. 7.1% of pairs admit no working design and 35.7% admit almost any, and two thirds of the variance in the ON-state opening cost is between pairs. But only 2–14% of the variance in the leak and in every contrast is — 86–98% of it is within a pair. The pair decides whether there is anything to work with; the levers decide how well it discriminates. Two screens, not one.
  7. Trigger A's grip on the secondary arm has a threshold, not a trend. Weaker than about −15 to −17 kcal/mol the leak collapses; stronger than that it is saturated and further tightening changes nothing. Design to clear the line, not to maximise the term.
  8. The secondary stem's energies are trigger B's sequence, and mostly its GC. Each stem energy is predicted by its own trigger's accessibility (ρ +0.283 and +0.304) and lock_energy by trigger B's alone (+0.320 against B, +0.036 and not significant against A) — because the arm is built from x*, which is read out of gene B. Holding each trigger's GC fixed collapses the A-side link to +0.041, so what looks like an accessibility effect is composition propagating from the transcript into the switch. Accessibility and GC are not two independent screens on a trigger window.
  9. Kim's extended secondary arm buys nothing measurable here. Over 140,256 matched design pairs it wins no contrast at significance, the median paired difference is 0.000 on all four, and a third of trigger pairs are exactly tied. The extra geometry is not where the problem is.

D7 · What we would change next, in order

  1. Stop screening trigger windows on accessibility and GC as if they were two screens. §R9 shows the part of accessibility that predicts a stem energy is almost all its GC part — entirely so on the A side. Decide which of the two the selection stage uses, and then re-run the same mediation test on the outcome rather than on the stem energy: l_local_min against the cell's best A_M_11 with GC held fixed, which is the one link in §R9 we have not taken apart.
  2. Measure a barrier along the invasion path in state 11. D2's negative result about trigger A's grip is a statement about four equilibrium observables and one switch-only barrier. The quantity that would test it directly is the saddle on the path from the closed switch&A&B complex to the open one, which FoldEngine.saddle cannot compute as written because findpath takes a single strand. That is a bounded piece of work, and until it exists the claim stays a claim about what we measure.
  3. Decide which accessibility measure is canonical — l_local_* or access_* — and retire the other, rather than filling in the empty one (§R10).
  4. Adopt the worst-of-all-three OFF convention across every family, and retire the state-00-referenced ied_gain as a selection metric — keeping the ensemble defect itself, which works (D3).
  5. Widen the trigger-pair sample before generalising any lever conclusion, and widen it to gene pairs that do not all share ggt.
